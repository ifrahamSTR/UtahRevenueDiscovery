Where do Utah's strongest short-term rentals concentrate?

Built from AirDNA/USPPD data (11,386 Utah listings), this tool visualizes where the state's strongest short-term rentals concentrate — it does not pick a market or build a buy box. See Methodology for filters and sourcing.

Section 2 · the centerpiece of this page

The Map

Every dot is one listing, plotted at its real coordinates. Green marks listings at or above the revenue threshold; size within that green group scales with how far above the threshold a listing sits, so an exceptional outlier reads as bigger, not just "qualifying." Everything else recedes to a small, muted gray dot — still visible, because the surrounding market density is part of the story, but out of the way. Use the threshold slider, the Actual/Potential toggle, and the filters to test what's producing the pattern you see; use Inspect area (top-left of the map) to drop a radius of up to 50 km anywhere — a ~5 mile (8 km) first-pass scale is the default — and get a full local market summary: hit rate vs. the statewide baseline, revenue/ADR/occupancy medians, property mix, amenities, and the strongest listings nearby, rather than guessing from dot density alone. Click any listing for its full detail card — the Airbnb link is a button inside it, never an automatic redirect.

Section 3 · discovered, not assumed

Natural Revenue Regions

These 12 regions were not drawn by hand and don't follow city limits, ZIP codes, or AirDNA's own market boundaries. Each one is a data-driven cluster of listings at or above $90,000 actual revenue that sit close together geographically (density-based clustering, adjacent pockets merged, then grown by 15 km to capture the full surrounding market) — see Methodology for the exact method. A region's size reflects how many strong listings cluster there, not a target headcount; some are tight double-figure pockets, and that's the point. Open any region for its own map, findings, and STR regulatory research.

Section 4

Findings: What's Behind the Green Dots

These are supporting observations to help interpret the map — not a ranking, and not a recommendation. Every statistic below is computed directly from the same working dataset, at a fixed reference threshold.

Rate, Not Occupancy, Separates the Top of the Market

Listings at or above the threshold aren't filling more nights than everyone else — they're charging far more per night, and they tend to be considerably larger properties. The chart below indexes each metric's median for the $90k+ group against the median for everyone below it (1.0× = no difference).

Property Type & Setting

Houses and cabins make up the large majority of $90k+ listings, both converting at a noticeably higher rate than condos, apartments, or townhouses. Geographically, "Destination/Resort" listings (AirDNA's mountain/lake location type) hold a similarly outsized share — roughly three times the hit rate of mid-size-city or suburban listings, and well over ten times the hit rate of listings in dense urban location types, even though resort listings aren't the largest slice of total inventory.

The Hot Tub Signal

Listings with a hot tub reach the $90k+ threshold at a far higher rate than those without — and the gap holds up even after splitting by property size, so it isn't purely a proxy for "bigger home." That said, correlation isn't causation: hot tubs are also far more common in destination/resort settings and on larger, higher-end properties, so some of this signal is likely shared with those factors rather than the amenity alone. Read it as a strong, investigable pattern — not a standalone rule.

Section 5

Methodology & Scope

  • Source: AirDNA/USPPD Utah property-level export, pulled via Snowflake (Snowflake/Utah/Iqbal_Idaho_2026-09-17-0204.csv — the filename is an inherited naming-template artifact; the data itself is confirmed Utah).
  • Working population: Entire home/apt listings in Utah with 20+ reviews and 230+ active listing nights in the trailing twelve months, minus 108 listings flagged by QC as boutique-hotel/event-venue properties rather than genuine single-unit STR comps — 11,386 listings.
  • Actual (LTM) revenue = REVENUE_LTM: real trailing-twelve-month gross revenue. Potential (LTM) revenue = REVENUE_POTENTIAL_LTM: AirDNA's modeled revenue under an optimized booking calendar.
  • $90,000 reference threshold: a starting point for visual emphasis and the Findings-section comparisons, adjustable live on the map — not a market cutoff or investment rule.
  • Region discovery: DBSCAN density clustering (haversine distance) over listings ≥ $90k actual revenue — eps=8km, min 4 listings per cluster — with clusters whose centroids sit within 25km merged into one region (tighter than the Idaho site's 12km/30km, re-tuned so Park City and Salt Lake City — genuinely different markets ~30km apart — don't get chained into one region by the urban corridor between them). Each region's boundary is the convex hull of its qualifying listings, buffered 15km outward to capture the surrounding market at every revenue level. Fully reproducible: scripts/generate_map_data.py. This finds concentrations of strong listings, not just dense listing counts — it will not simply rediscover Salt Lake City.
  • STR regulatory research (regional pages): sourced from primary government/statute text where available, dated at verification, with uncertainty explicitly flagged where a primary source could not be confirmed. Not legal advice — verify directly before relying on it for an acquisition or operating decision.
  • Scope: this is a revenue-side geographic discovery and research tool, the first trial of a workflow meant to generalize to other states. It does not select a market, define a buy box, or make an acquisition recommendation — those calls stay with the analyst.

Exploratory discovery tool. No market has been selected, no buy box has been defined, and no property has been underwritten. Revenue figures are AirDNA/USPPD LTM benchmarks, not a full underwriting model.
